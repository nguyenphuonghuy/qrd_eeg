\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:gioithieu}

A brain--computer interface (BCI) translates brain activity into control
signals for external devices without passing through the usual
neuromuscular pathways \cite{chen2025brain, tonin2021noninvasive,
ding2026brain}. Its applications have spread from the laboratory to
practical systems \cite{varbu2022present, pang2024applications} such as
powered wheelchairs \cite{palumbo2021motorimagery, lei2022intention,
mai2024hybrid}, assistive robotic arms and prostheses \cite{casey2021bci},
upper-limb rehabilitation devices \cite{baniqued2021brain}, and
intention-driven vehicles \cite{lu2020model}. This study focuses on motor
imagery (MI), a paradigm suited to sustained, self-paced control because
it needs no external stimulus \cite{palumbo2021motorimagery}. In these
systems, the EEG decoder is one stage of a closed control loop. Its
inference time must fit within the time budget of the loop
\cite{an2024development, tonin2020role}. The model must run on the
embedded hardware built into the device, where time, memory, and energy
must be considered together \cite{sun2025signal, akbari2025atrial,
craighero2024ondevice, wang2024mibminet, pacini2024design,
sen2025lowlatency}. Its reliability must remain
stable across sessions and subjects, especially when the BCI shares
control with an autonomous system \cite{choi2023asynchronous,
deng2020selfadaptive, wei2023intra}. The move toward consumer devices with
few channels further degrades signal quality \cite{wu2022deepbrain,
tarara2025motor, paneru2025eeg}. The paper uses the information transfer
rate (ITR) \cite{wolpaw2002brain} and the closed-loop time budget as
evaluation criteria alongside accuracy (Section~\ref{sec:rangbuoc}).

These requirements confront the designer of a motor imagery decoder with a conflict between accuracy and deployability. Deep learning
has substantially improved motor imagery decoding over hand-crafted
features \cite{al-saegh2021deep, sadiq2021development}. Compact
architectures such as EEGNet \cite{lawhern2018eegnet} and its variants
\cite{ingolfsson2020eegtcnet, deng2021advanced, wang2022mieeg,
wang2023ifnet} can be deployed on embedded hardware. Higher-capacity models range from deep convolutional networks
\cite{schirrmeister2017deep}, transformers, and hybrid architectures
\cite{vafaei2025transformers, zhao2024ctnet, ding2025eegdeformer,
altaheri2025temporal, wang20232d} to multi-component ensembles
\cite{zoumpourlis2024motor}. They are more accurate but costly at inference
and strongly dependent on the amount of training data
\cite{keutayeva2024data, keutayeva2024compact}. Knowledge distillation
(KD) targets this conflict directly by transferring knowledge from a large
teacher to a small student without increasing the inference cost of the
student \cite{hinton2015distilling}. KD is now one of the main strategies
for building compact EEG decoders \cite{li2025lightweight,
zhou2026structural}, alongside pruning, quantization, and hardware
acceleration \cite{xie2024comprehensive}.

Two gaps limit the value of existing comparisons of distillation
mechanisms for EEG. The first gap concerns how distillation mechanisms are compared with one another.
Training deep networks is affected by several sources of randomness, and
this effect can be larger on EEG data, which vary strongly both between
and within subjects \cite{huang2023discrepancy}. Changing the random seed
alone can produce substantial differences between training runs of the
same architecture \cite{picard2021torch}. Comparisons of machine learning
methods need to account for these sources of variance
\cite{bouthillier2021accounting}, and similar concerns have been raised for
deep learning on neural data \cite{delpup2024toward}. Even so, comparisons
among distillation mechanisms for EEG are still commonly reported from a
single run. The extent to which a single-run ranking reflects real
differences has hardly been measured directly. The second gap lies in the design of relational distillation mechanisms themselves. In existing work, the
space in which relations are measured and the way sample pairs are chosen
always change together. It is therefore impossible to tell whether an
observed effect comes from the representation space or from how relations
are organized.

Distillation methods are grouped by the form of transferred knowledge
into output-based, feature-based, and relation-based methods
\cite{gou2021knowledge, mansourian2025comprehensive}. Feature-based
methods use hints from hidden layers \cite{romero2015fitnets} and have
been applied to biomedical images \cite{javed2023knowledge}.
Relation-based methods represent knowledge through distances and angles
between representation vectors \cite{park2019relational}, pairwise
similarity \cite{feng2021double}, local neighborhood relations
\cite{gou2025neighborhood}, or recursively constructed relations
\cite{ye2021unsupervised}. These groups have been extended to self-distillation
\cite{zhang2022selfdistillation, mai2025eegskdnet} and to physiological
signals \cite{wang2021fldnet, gu2023framelevel, zhang2023distilling,
ibrahim2022knowledge, garcia-salinas2023intrasubject}. For EEG, relations among samples appear particularly
suitable because discriminative information often lies in the relative
structure among samples. Similarity-Keeping Knowledge Distillation preserves the similarity
structure when moving to a system with fewer electrodes
\cite{huang2024enhancing}. Multi-level distillation frameworks have been
proposed for motor imagery \cite{wu2026multilevel}, and an ensemble teacher
has been distilled into a single backbone under latency constraints
\cite{xu2026entropy}. In these works, the sample pairs used to
build relations are mostly chosen empirically, and relations are always
measured in a classical metric space.

Quantum machine learning offers a fundamentally different space for
measuring relations. A parameterized quantum circuit maps data into a
Hilbert space, where the inner product between two states acts as a kernel
function \cite{schuld2019quantum, schuld2020circuitcentric,
jerbi2023quantum}. A kernel that is hard to compute classically does not
by itself imply a learning advantage \cite{huang2021power}. The performance of a quantum kernel also depends strongly on its feature
map and hyperparameters \cite{jha2026comparative}. Exploiting such kernels
is still limited by the qubit count, circuit depth, and noise of current
hardware \cite{xie2025advances, alam2022qnet}. In biomedicine and EEG, the quantum component is usually inserted into
the backbone or placed at the end of the processing chain
\cite{idzikowski2026survey, chen2026exploring, olvera2024eegbased,
behera2026qsvmqnn, dutta2026duoqepinet}. Designs in other fields follow the
same pattern
\cite{wang2022development, griol-barres2021variational, qi2024qtnvqc}.

Quantum knowledge distillation has been used to compress quantum networks
\cite{yan2026distilling} and to synchronize distributed quantum models
\cite{oh2025quantum}. It has also transferred knowledge from a classical
teacher to a quantum student \cite{nishan2026quantummedkd,
barbato2026quantum} and let a classical network emulate quantum dynamics
\cite{yao2023emulating}. In the compression and quantum-student
directions, the quantum component remains in the inference model. As in
the EEG designs above, the deployed model must then access
quantum hardware or a simulator at run time, which conflicts with the
latency and resource constraints. Quantum Relational Knowledge
Distillation (QRKD) and Quantum-Train use the quantum circuit only during training \cite{liu2025quantum,
liu2025quantumtrain}. Their premise is that quantum kernels describe
relations among representations differently from classical kernels
\cite{liu2022representation}. This
approach suits a decoder in a control loop, but QRKD has only been
verified on vision and language with a fixed relation structure.

This study builds a structured relational distillation framework (QRD) to
address both gaps under tightly controlled conditions. The QRD framework
treats the relation-formation strategy and the space used to measure
similarity as two separate design axes and varies one axis at a time.
The interaction between the two axes is tested separately with a factorial experiment.
Five relation strategies are examined, ranging from dense relations and
sparse relations based on representative samples to selection guided by
the uncertainty of the teacher. They are combined with cosine, RBF, and
quantum kernels on the same loss function, with every other component kept
fixed. The quantum kernel
is not included as a proposed solution. It is the strictest test case,
because the geometry of Hilbert space cannot be reduced to Euclidean
distance in the original feature space. Every relation kernel
participates only in computing the loss, so the model deployed into the
control loop remains a compact classical network.

The research contribution is a controlled evaluation protocol for
distillation mechanisms on motor imagery EEG, together with quantitative
evidence that random-seed noise exceeds the differences among mechanisms.
It comprises the following three elements, in order of importance.

\begin{itemize}
\item The main contribution is the evidence of seed instability. It was
obtained with a protocol that uses the subject as the statistical unit,
five fixed seeds, and a statistical plan fixed before running. Seed noise
(about 0.75 percentage points) is larger than the differences among
mechanisms (about 0.46 points). The ranking from a single run is almost
uncorrelated with the ranking over five seeds (Spearman coefficient 0.14).

\item The QRD framework is the controlled test bed for this evidence.
Within it, distillation provides a stable benefit on PhysionetMI and
Cho2017. However, no relation variant or kernel, including the quantum
kernel, separates from the KDBase baseline at statistical significance.

\item Every configuration is additionally evaluated from a
control-oriented perspective, using ITR, the inference-time budget, and a
real inference-time measurement on embedded hardware.
\end{itemize}

\section{Method}
\label{sec:phuongphap}

\subsection{Research workflow and overview of the QRD framework}
\label{sec:tongquan}

Figure~\ref{fig:quytrinh} summarizes the research workflow, from data
to statistical conclusions. The signals are first preprocessed and the
103 PhysionetMI subjects are divided into five folds by a deterministic
rule that does not depend on the seed (Section~\ref{sec:dulieu}). The
teacher is then trained once per fold and kept fixed. For each
combination of seed, fold, and distillation configuration, the student
is trained and its checkpoint is selected by validation accuracy. The
per-subject results are aggregated into one value per seed before
testing according to the pre-specified plan. Finally, the same workflow
is replicated exploratorily on Cho2017, and the inference time of the
student is measured on a Raspberry Pi 4. Section~\ref{sec:pipeline}
presents this workflow as pseudocode (Algorithm~2). The rest of this
part focuses on the QRD framework, that is, the configuration block $c$
and the QRD branch in Figure~\ref{fig:quytrinh}.

This part describes how the components of the QRD framework connect
to one another and defines the role of each component during
training. Figure~\ref{fig:khung} shows the entire architecture.

The framework is organized as a teacher--student setup. The teacher
is a pretrained ensemble model \cite{zoumpourlis2024motor}, kept
fixed and serving only as the source of the supervisory signal. The
student is an EEGNet network \cite{lawhern2018eegnet} and is the only
component in the teacher--student pair whose parameters are updated
\cite{hinton2015distilling}. The exception concerning the quantum
circuit shared between the two branches is discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:qrd}.

This pair of models was chosen to concretely realize the conflict
noted above. The ensemble model aggregates information from multiple
components and therefore provides a stable supervisory signal.
However, its memory and computational cost increases with the number
of components, making it poorly suited to the embedded hardware
built into the device. EEGNet, conversely, has a parameter count and
inference time suited to embedded hardware and has been verified in
practically deployed BCI systems \cite{wang2024mibminet}. The
performance gap between the two models is precisely the quantity
that the distillation framework seeks to narrow.

The supervisory signal delivered to the student consists of a base component that matches the soft prediction distribution at the logits level. Depending on the configuration, at most one of three additional components is added. The general loss function for this
combination is written in \eqref{eq:total}, with the coefficients of
the components not belonging to the configuration under
consideration set to zero. The first two additional components
belong to classical distillation, comprising alignment of
intermediate features and preservation of the geometric structure
among samples in feature space. Both are presented in
Section~\ref{sec:cocdien} and used as the basis for comparison.

The third additional component is the quantum relation branch, the component that this study adds. This branch takes the intermediate
features of both models, constructs a relation matrix in Hilbert
space, and produces an additional loss component. It exists only in
the computational graph of the training stage, does not appear in
the inference path, and therefore does not change the configuration
of the deployed model.

The remainder of this part establishes the notation used
consistently throughout Section~\ref{sec:phuongphap}.
Consider a mini-batch of $N$ EEG samples $\{x_i\}_{i=1}^{N}$. For each sample, each model produces two types of output. These are the logit vectors $z_T(x_i), z_S(x_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{C}$ at the classification layer and the feature vectors $f_T(x_i), f_S(x_i) \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$ at the last representation layer before it.
For the pair of models used, $C = 2$ and $d = 144$ for both the
teacher and the student.

Stacking the feature vectors by column gives the feature matrix of
the mini-batch, matching the convention used in Figure~\ref{fig:khung}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:featmat}
F_T = \begin{bmatrix} f_T(x_1) & \cdots & f_T(x_N) \end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N},
\qquad
F_S = \begin{bmatrix} f_S(x_1) & \cdots & f_S(x_N) \end{bmatrix}
\in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}.
\end{equation}

Distillation at the relational level does not compare $F_T$ with
$F_S$ directly but compares the relation structure among samples
within each model. This structure is represented by two symmetric matrices $R_T$ and $R_S$. Element $(i,j)$ of each matrix measures the similarity between samples $x_i$ and $x_j$ according to the representation of the corresponding model. In classical relational
distillation methods, these two matrices are constructed from
Euclidean distance or cosine similarity in feature space
\cite{park2019relational}. Section~\ref{sec:qrd} replaces that space
with a Hilbert space, in which similarity is measured by a quantum
kernel function.

The relation matrix is not necessarily computed over the entire
mini-batch. The set of participating index pairs, denoted
$\mathcal{I}$, is a configuration parameter and is defined
separately for each variant in Section~\ref{sec:bienthe}.

\subsection{Classical distillation setups}
\label{sec:cocdien}

This part defines three classical distillation configurations used
as baselines. All three share the same teacher, student, dataset,
and optimization protocol. The only component that changes is the
form of the additional supervisory signal. Because of this
constraint, any observed performance difference can be attributed to
the knowledge-transfer mechanism.

\textbf{KDBase} performs distillation at the output level, matching
the student's soft prediction distribution to the teacher's through
temperature-softened Kullback--Leibler divergence
\cite{hinton2015distilling}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kd}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}} = \tau^{2}\,
  \mathrm{KL}\!\left(
  \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{z_T}{\tau}\right) \,\middle\|\,
  \sigma\!\left(\tfrac{z_S}{\tau}\right)\right),
\end{equation}

where $\sigma(\cdot)$ is the softmax function and $\tau = 4.0$ is the
softening temperature. The coefficient $\tau^{2}$ keeps the gradient
magnitude independent of $\tau$.

\textbf{FeatureKD} adds a constraint at the intermediate feature
level \cite{romero2015fitnets}. Because the student and teacher have
the same feature dimensionality, the constraint is applied directly
without an intermediate projection layer:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:feat}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{feat}} = \frac{1}{Nd}
  \left\| F_S - F_T \right\|_{F}^{2}.
\end{equation}

\textbf{RelationalKD} shifts the focus from individual samples to
relations among samples \cite{park2019relational}. This study uses
the pairwise-distance component of the original method. For each
model, the pairwise Euclidean distance matrix is normalized by the
mean of its off-diagonal elements:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:pdist}
\tilde{D}_{ij} = \frac{\|f_i - f_j\|_2}
  {\dfrac{1}{N(N-1)}\displaystyle\sum_{p \neq q} \|f_p - f_q\|_2},
\end{equation}

and the discrepancy between the two normalized matrices is measured
by the Huber function:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:rkd}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}} = \ell_{\delta}\!\left(
  \tilde{D}^{S}, \tilde{D}^{T} \right),
\end{equation}

where $\ell_{\delta}$ is the smooth L1 loss function. Normalizing by the mean makes the loss function invariant to uniform scaling of feature space. The student is therefore required to reproduce only the relative structure of the teacher's representation, not its absolute magnitude.

The total loss function of any configuration has the form

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:total}
\mathcal{L} = \alpha\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}
  + (1-\alpha)\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{KD}}
  + \gamma_{f}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{feat}}
  + \gamma_{r}\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{rel}}
  + \beta\,\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{CE}}$ is the cross-entropy with the true
labels, and the coefficients of the components not belonging to the
configuration under consideration are set to zero. The component
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$ is presented in Section~\ref{sec:qrd}.
The coefficient values are given in Section~\ref{sec:cauhinh}.

The three configurations above form a sequence of increasing
abstraction in the type of knowledge transferred, from output to
individual features to relations among samples. RelationalKD is the
first configuration in the sequence to represent knowledge in
relational form, and it is also the point that the QRD framework
extends. Instead of measuring relations by Euclidean distance in
feature space, QRD measures them by a kernel function in Hilbert
space.

\subsection{Quantum relational distillation framework}
\label{sec:qrd}

The quantum kernel function is included to extend the scope of the
conclusions rather than as a proposed solution
(Section~\ref{sec:gioithieu}). The classical relation strategies all
construct structure within the same metric space. If only these were
compared with one another, a negative result could be attributed to
the representation space not actually having been changed.

The quantum relation branch consists of three steps: dimensionality
reduction and encoding of features into rotation angles, quantum
state preparation, and kernel computation to construct the relation
matrix.

\textbf{Dimensionality reduction and angle encoding.} The
$d = 144$-dimensional feature vector cannot be encoded directly into
an $n_q = 4$-qubit circuit. Each model is assigned its own linear
projection $W_T, W_S : \mathbb{R}^{d} \to \mathbb{R}^{n_q}$. The
teacher-side projection $W_T$ is initialized and kept fixed
throughout training. Only the student-side projection $W_S$ is the
trainable parameter among these two projections. Variant V1 in
Section~\ref{sec:bienthe} replaces $W_S$ with a deterministic
dimensionality reduction, in which case the student-side quantum
branch also has no trainable parameter at the projection step.

The projection $W_T$ is initialized within the same global random
number stream, so it is likely to differ across seeds. The effect of
this variation is checked with a control that fixes $W_T$
(Appendix~\ref{app:doichung}). Initialization details and the
theoretical limits of the projection are given in
Appendix~\ref{app:caidat}. The output $Wf(x_i)$ of the projection is
not bounded in magnitude, whereas the rotation angle used for the
quantum gate needs to lie within a defined range. Therefore, this
output is bounded into the valid angle range using the hyperbolic
tangent function before being used as the rotation angle:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:angles}
a(x_i) = \pi \, \tanh\!\big( W f(x_i) \big) \in [-\pi, \pi]^{n_q}.
\end{equation}

This bounding limits the rotation angle to a full period, reducing
the risk that two different feature values map to the same state due
to the periodicity of rotation.

\textbf{State preparation.} The angle vector is loaded into the circuit via angle encoding around the $Y$ axis, denoted $\Phi(\cdot)$. The encoding alternates with $L = 2$ Strongly Entangling Layers of entangling gates \cite{schuld2020circuitcentric}, denoted $U(\theta_l)$ for the $l$-th layer. Unlike a single-pass encode-then-entangle circuit, the
encoding is repeated, alternating with the entangling layers, in a
\emph{data re-uploading} style \cite{perezsalinas2020data}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:layer}
\ket{\varphi_l(x_i)} = U(\theta_l)\,\Phi\!\big(a(x_i)\big)\,\ket{\varphi_{l-1}(x_i)},
\qquad
\ket{\varphi_0(x_i)} = \ket{0}^{\otimes n_q},
\qquad l = 1,\ldots,L,
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:state}
\ket{\psi(x_i)} = \Phi\!\big(a(x_i)\big)\,\ket{\varphi_L(x_i)}.
\end{equation}

For $L=2$, the full gate sequence is encode--entangle--encode--entangle--encode.
The data is re-loaded three times, alternating with two parametrized
entangling layers. The parameters $\theta = (\theta_1,\ldots,\theta_L)$
are initialized once from the normal distribution $\mathcal{N}(0, 0.1)$.
They are trainable parameters, updated via backpropagation
together with $W_S$ throughout training. The circuit $U(\theta_l)$
uses a single shared set $\theta$ for both the teacher
branch and the student branch, without separating $\theta_T$/$\theta_S$.
The consequence of this choice for the teacher's ``fixed'' status is
analyzed later in this part.

Training $\theta$ is only meaningful if the entangling layer
$U(\theta_l)$ actually affects the kernel function rather than
canceling itself out of the computation. This risk is real in a
single-pass encode-then-entangle circuit,
$\ket{\psi(x_i)} = U_\theta\,\Phi(a(x_i))\,\ket{0}^{\otimes n_q}$,
where the entangling layer $U_\theta$ is an operator that is
\emph{identical for every sample} and lies outermost in the
expression. The kernel then depends only on the inner product
$\braket{\psi(x_i)|\psi(x_j)} = \bra{0}\Phi(a(x_i))^\dagger U_\theta^\dagger U_\theta \Phi(a(x_j))\ket{0}$. Because $U_\theta^\dagger U_\theta = I$ for every $\theta$, the entangling layer cancels out of the kernel regardless of how it is trained, and the circuit degenerates into a classical kernel function of $\Phi$ alone.

The data re-uploading structure in \eqref{eq:layer}--\eqref{eq:state}
avoids this degeneracy. The final encoding $\Phi(a(x_i))$ is a
\emph{sample-dependent} operator and lies outermost instead of
$U_\theta$, so the same reduction no longer applies. $U(\theta_l)$
cannot be removed from the inner product by a data-independent
unitarity argument. Empirical verification on a fixed batch gives a difference of $0.474$ between the quantum kernel and the corresponding cosine product (Appendix~\ref{app:caidat}). The circuit therefore does not degenerate into a classical kernel.

The circuit is then no longer a fixed random feature map into the
$2^{n_q}$-dimensional Hilbert space as in a single-pass encoding
design, but a \emph{trainable} feature map. The data-adaptive
capacity of the quantum branch lies in $W_S$ and $\theta$. $W_T$
remains fixed as noted above.

\textbf{Kernel function and relation matrix.} The similarity between
two samples is measured by the fidelity between their corresponding
states \cite{schuld2019quantum}:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:kernel}
K\!\big(a_i, a_j\big) =
  \big| \braket{ \psi(x_i) | \psi(x_j) } \big|^{2} \in [0,1].
\end{equation}

Applying this separately to each model gives two quantum relation
matrices:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:relmat}
R_T^{(Q)}(i,j) = K\!\big(a_T(x_i), a_T(x_j)\big),
\qquad
R_S^{(Q)}(i,j) = K\!\big(a_S(x_i), a_S(x_j)\big),
\end{equation}

where $a_T$ and $a_S$ are the angle vectors obtained from
\eqref{eq:angles} with the corresponding projection. The two matrices
are used directly without any additional normalization step, because
the kernel already takes values in $[0,1]$ by definition.

\textbf{Loss function.} The discrepancy between the two relation
structures is measured by the mean squared error over the index-pair
set $\mathcal{I}$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:qrd}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}} = \frac{1}{|\mathcal{I}|}
  \sum_{(i,j) \in \mathcal{I}}
  \left( R_T^{(Q)}(i,j) - R_S^{(Q)}(i,j) \right)^{2}.
\end{equation}

The gradient is propagated through the quantum circuit to the projection $W_S$ and continues into the student network. This component therefore affects the student's entire learning process, not only the projection layer. The projection $W_T$ participates in computing
$R_T^{(Q)}$ but does not receive gradients, as noted above.

However, $R_T^{(Q)}$ is not entirely static. The computation of
$R_T^{(Q)}$ is not detached from the computational graph. No
\texttt{detach} or \texttt{no\_grad} step wraps the teacher branch in
\eqref{eq:qrd}, so the gradient of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$ still
propagates to $\theta$ through both the teacher branch and the
student branch. The teacher's backbone and the projection $W_T$
remain fixed, but the mapping from the teacher's features to
$R_T^{(Q)}$ still changes with $\theta$ throughout training. In the classical distillation setups of Section~\ref{sec:cocdien}, by contrast, the teacher target is entirely fixed.

In principle, sharing $\theta$ opens up an alternative optimization
direction for \eqref{eq:qrd}. Training could shift $\theta$ to make the two matrices easier to match, for example by making both kernels flatter. The student would then no longer be forced to reproduce the exact relation structure that the teacher has learned. This is a different
form of degeneracy from the classical-kernel degeneracy caused by the
cancellation of $U_\theta$ examined above. Separating a fixed $\theta_T$ from a trainable $\theta_S$ would avoid this risk. In exchange, the two branches would no longer be measured by the same kernel function, which would undermine the basis for directly
comparing $R_T^{(Q)}$ with $R_S^{(Q)}$ in \eqref{eq:qrd}. This study
chooses to share $\theta$ and examines this trade-off with the
control in Appendix~\ref{app:doichung}.

In the total objective function \eqref{eq:total}, the QRD
configurations use $\gamma_f = \gamma_r = 0$. That is, the student
simultaneously receives supervisory signal from the true labels, from
the teacher's logits, and from the quantum relation structure. The coefficient $\beta$ is two orders of magnitude smaller than the other coefficients. The reason is that $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$ is a discrepancy between two quantities bounded in $[0,1]$, so its magnitude is much smaller than that of the other loss components.

The same $\beta = 0.02$ is used for all three kernel function types
compared in Section~\ref{sec:bienthe} (quantum, cosine, RBF). That all three kernels give values in $[0,1]$ only guarantees that the scale of $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$ lies within the same range. It does not guarantee that the three kernels produce the same gradient magnitude or the same influence on the student when multiplied by the same $\beta$. RBF self-normalizes according
to the median distance of each batch, whereas the quantum kernel
tends to concentrate values near the boundary because of the
geometry of Hilbert space. More generally, the value distribution of a quantum kernel depends strongly on the embedding. Kernel values are known to concentrate as embeddings become more expressive \cite{thanasilp2024exponential}. Their effective scale is set by the encoding bandwidth \cite{shaydulin2022importance}. The actual
difference in supervisory magnitude among the three kernels is
measured and addressed in Section~\ref{sec:chuyensau}.

\subsection{Variants of the QRD framework}
\label{sec:bienthe}

The five variants are built on the same quantum kernel function
\eqref{eq:kernel} and the same loss function \eqref{eq:qrd}. They differ in how the $d$-dimensional feature is reduced to $n_q$ dimensions before quantum encoding. They also differ in the index set $\mathcal{I}$ that determines which sample pairs participate in the relation matrix. Figure~\ref{fig:bienthe} illustrates the five
designs.

This design stems from the observation that not every sample pair
carries the same amount of information. EEG signals have a low
signal-to-noise ratio and vary strongly across subjects
\cite{huang2023discrepancy}, so a dense
relation matrix may contain many pairs that reflect noise more than
class structure.

\begin{figure}[H]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figs/fig2_QRD.pdf}
\caption{The five variants of the QRD framework.}
\label{fig:bienthe}
\end{figure}
\FloatBarrier

Variants V1 through V4 share one implementation point that needs to
be stated first. The cost of simulating the quantum circuit grows quadratically with the number of samples. The relation matrix for these four variants is therefore computed not on the entire mini-batch of size $N$ but on a \emph{position-fixed} subsample. This subsample
consists of the first $q = \min(16, N)$ elements of each batch.
Because the batch has already been shuffled randomly, this subsample
is equivalent to a content-independent random sampling. Every index
set for V1 through V4 below is understood to be defined over those
$q$ samples.

\textbf{V1: dense relations, deterministic dimensionality
reduction.} This base variant does not use a learned linear
projection at the dimensionality-reduction step. The quantum circuit
$\theta$ is still trained as in every other variant
(Section~\ref{sec:qrd}), so this variant has 24 additional parameters (only $\theta$). The other four variants have 604 (580 from $W_S$ plus 24 from $\theta$). The
$d$-dimensional feature vector is divided evenly into $n_q$
consecutive groups, each averaged:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:chunk}
\big[g(f)\big]_m = \frac{n_q}{d}
  \sum_{l = (m-1)d/n_q + 1}^{m\,d/n_q} f_l,
  \qquad m = 1,\ldots,n_q,
\end{equation}

and then $g(f)$ replaces $W f$ in \eqref{eq:angles}. The index set
consists of all pairs:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:i-v1}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V1}} = \{(i,j) \mid 1 \le i,j \le q\}.
\end{equation}

This variant is the most direct extension of classical relational
distillation into Hilbert space and serves as the comparison point
for the other four variants.

\textbf{V2: dense relations, learned projection.} This variant keeps
V1's index set but replaces the deterministic dimensionality
reduction with the two linear projections $W_T$ and $W_S$ described in Section~\ref{sec:qrd}. Only $W_S$ is a trainable parameter, while $W_T$ remains fixed:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:i-v2}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V2}} = \mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V1}}.
\end{equation}

The pair V1 and V2 compares two designs for projecting features into angle space. V1 uses a fixed, parameter-free averaging, whereas V2 uses a pair of linear projections of which only the student-side $W_S$ is trained. This comparison changes more than the trainability of $W_S$
alone. Moving from V1 to V2 also replaces the teacher's deterministic
chunk-averaged target with a random (frozen) linear projection
$W_T$. Instantiating the two additional linear layers in V2 also
consumes extra random draws relative to V1's parameter-free
averaging. The V1-versus-V2 comparison is therefore best read as a comparison between two overall dimensionality-reduction designs. It is not a controlled ablation that isolates the benefit or cost of a learnable $W_S$ on its own. A causal claim specific to $W_S$ would
require a control that holds the teacher-side target representation
and the random draws fixed between the two variants.

\textbf{V3: sparse relations based on an anchor set.} To reduce
relation density, this variant restricts the index set to an anchor
set $\mathcal{A}$ of size $M = 8$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:i-v3}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V3}} = \{(a,a') \mid a, a' \in \mathcal{A}\},
\qquad \mathcal{A} \subset \{1,\ldots,q\}, \; |\mathcal{A}| = M.
\end{equation}

The anchor set is the first $M$ elements of the subsample. The
sample order is reshuffled at every epoch, so this is a random
selection not based on sample content. This choice is deliberate, isolating the effect of
\emph{reducing density} from the effect of \emph{guided selection},
which is investigated in V5. Samples outside $\mathcal{A}$ do not
contribute to the relation loss component, although they still
participate in the other components of \eqref{eq:total}.

\textbf{V4: combining global and local relations.} This variant adds
neighborhood relations to the anchor block:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:i-v4}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V4}} = \{(a,a') \mid a,a' \in \mathcal{A}\}
  \cup \{(i,j) \mid j \in \mathcal{N}_k(i)\},
\end{equation}

where $\mathcal{N}_k(i)$ is the set of the $k = 4$ nearest neighbors
of sample $x_i$. Neighbors are determined by Euclidean distance on
the teacher's features, that is, the local structure is taken from
the already-trained model rather than from the student that is still
learning. The resulting relation is symmetrized. If $j$ belongs to
the neighborhood of $i$, both pairs $(i,j)$ and $(j,i)$ belong to
$\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V4}}$. Unlike V3, every sample in the subsample
participates through its own neighborhood edges. Because
$\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V4}}$ is defined as a set, a pair $(i,j)$ that
appears in both the anchor block and the kNN block is counted
exactly once in \eqref{eq:qrd}, not accumulated.

\textbf{V5: teacher-guided relation selection.} The four variants
above select samples independently of content, on a fixed subset of
$q$ elements. V5 replaces that fixed subset with a subset
\emph{selected by content}, with the candidate pool being the entire
$N$ samples of the batch rather than the first $q$ elements. For
each sample $x_i$ in the entire batch ($i = 1,\ldots,N$), the
teacher's uncertainty is measured by the normalized entropy of the
prediction distribution:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:entropy}
u_i = -\frac{1}{\log C}\sum_{c=1}^{C}
  p_T(c \mid x_i) \log p_T(c \mid x_i),
\end{equation}

where $C$ is the number of classes, so that $u_i \in [0,1]$. The
degree of disagreement between the teacher and the student is
measured by the Jensen--Shannon divergence:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:js}
d_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_T \,\|\, m_i\right)
    + \tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{KL}\!\left(p_S \,\|\, m_i\right),
\qquad m_i = \tfrac{1}{2}\left(p_T + p_S\right),
\end{equation}

where $p_T$ and $p_S$ are shorthand for $p_T(\cdot \mid x_i)$ and
$p_S(\cdot \mid x_i)$. Compared with the $L_2$ norm, the
Jensen--Shannon divergence has the advantage of being symmetric,
bounded, and suited to comparing two probability distributions. The
combined score is a weighted average of the two quantities, computed
for all $N$ samples:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:score}
s_i = w_e\,u_i + w_d\,d_i, \qquad w_e = w_d = 0.5,
\qquad i = 1,\ldots,N.
\end{equation}

The set $\mathcal{S}$ consists of the $q$ samples with the highest
scores selected from the entire $N$ samples. The relation matrix is
constructed on the pairs within that subset of size $q$:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:i-v5}
\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V5}} = \{(i,j) \mid i,j \in \mathcal{S}\},
\qquad \mathcal{S} = \operatorname*{arg\,top\text{-}q}_{i \in \{1,\ldots,N\}} s_i.
\end{equation}

Unlike V1--V4, where the index set $\mathcal{I}$ is determined by a
fixed position or the teacher's frozen features, the selection here is data-dependent. The set $\mathcal{S}$ depends on the score $s_i$ through a discrete, non-differentiable selection. Meanwhile, the step of
constructing the relation matrix on $\mathcal{S}$ still requires
normal backpropagation. Algorithm~1 summarizes the computation
sequence and clarifies the gradient boundary between these two
parts.

\begin{quote}
\ttfamily\small\raggedright
\noindent\textbf{Algorithm 1. Teacher-guided relation selection (V5).}\\[2pt]
\textbf{Input:} teacher logits $Z_T \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}$,
student logits $Z_S \in \mathbb{R}^{C \times N}$, teacher/student
features $F_T, F_S \in \mathbb{R}^{d \times N}$, selection set size
$q$.\\
\textbf{Output:} the $q \times q$ quantum relation matrices used for
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$.\\[4pt]
1: \quad \# Steps 1--3 lie outside the gradient graph (torch.no\_grad)\\
2: \quad \textbf{for} $i = 1,\ldots,N$: compute $u_i$ via
\eqref{eq:entropy} from $Z_T$\\
3: \quad \textbf{for} $i = 1,\ldots,N$: compute $d_i$ via
\eqref{eq:js} from $Z_T, Z_S$\\
4: \quad \textbf{for} $i = 1,\ldots,N$: $s_i \leftarrow w_e u_i + w_d d_i$
(\eqref{eq:score})\\
5: \quad $\mathcal{S} \leftarrow$ indices of the $q$ largest values
of $s_i$ among the $N$ samples (\texttt{torch.topk}, no
backpropagation)\\[4pt]
6: \quad \# From here, gradients are reconnected normally\\
7: \quad $F_T[\mathcal{S}],\, F_S[\mathcal{S}] \leftarrow$ extract
the $q$ columns of $F_T, F_S$ at indices $\mathcal{S}$\\
8: \quad Encode $F_T[\mathcal{S}], F_S[\mathcal{S}]$ into quantum
states via \eqref{eq:angles}--\eqref{eq:state}\\
9: \quad Construct $R_T^{(Q)}, R_S^{(Q)} \in \mathbb{R}^{q \times q}$
via \eqref{eq:kernel}--\eqref{eq:relmat} on the $q$ selected samples\\
10: \quad Compute $\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$ via \eqref{eq:qrd} on
these two $q \times q$ matrices
\end{quote}

The candidate pool in lines 2--4 is the entire $N$ samples, so the
\texttt{top-q} operation in line 5 is a genuine selection that
discards the $N - q$ lower-scoring samples. V1--V4, in contrast,
perform no selection once the first $q$ samples have been fixed by
position. V5 therefore differs from V2--V4 in both the selection
criterion and the candidate pool. Output-level information only
guides sample selection, through a discrete rule fixed by
\eqref{eq:score}. The relation matrix is still constructed from the
intermediate features and fully participates in backpropagation
(lines 6--10).

The five variants form two intersecting survey axes. The first axis,

\subsection{Data, preprocessing, and evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:dulieu}

The method is evaluated on the PhysioNet EEG Motor Movement/Imagery
dataset \cite{schalk2004bci2000, goldberger2000physiobank}, referred to as
PhysionetMI after the name of its dataset class in MOABB. It contains EEG
recordings from 109 subjects acquired with a 64-electrode system under the
10--10 standard at a sampling frequency of 160 Hz. Following the exclusion
list of the teacher work \cite{zoumpourlis2024motor}, six subjects with
recordings inconsistent in duration or label assignment were excluded
(codes 88, 90, 92, 100, 104, and 106). The remaining 103 subjects were used.

The experiments focus on the two-class problem \emph{left\_hand} versus
\emph{right\_hand}, the default two-class paradigm of the
\texttt{PhysionetMI} class in MOABB. It distinguishes imagined left-hand and
right-hand grasping, two basic control commands in brain-signal-operated
assistive systems. The three remaining classes
of the original dataset (rest, feet, hands) were not used. The
number of trials per subject is not fixed, varying according to the
actual number of recordings retained. For each subject, the \emph{training} trials are undersampled per class
to the smaller of the two class counts, by random sampling without
replacement drawn from the training seed. The training set is therefore
class-balanced subject by subject. The random seed thus also controls
which training trials are kept, in addition to weight initialization and
batch order. The validation and test partitions keep every trial of the
selected classes and are not rebalanced.

The raw 160-Hz signal is processed in four sequential steps. It is
bandpass filtered at 4--38 Hz with a zero-phase FIR filter under the
default MNE/braindecode configuration, without explicit order tuning. It is
then re-referenced to the common average, resampled to 100 Hz, and
windowed. There is no notch filtering
step and no explicit artifact-removal step (no ICA, no
trial-rejection threshold). Each epoch begins exactly at the event
onset, with no start or end offset delay, and lasts 3.0 s,
corresponding to 300 samples at 100 Hz after resampling.

After windowing and the train/validation/test split described
below, a fifth step is applied: Riemannian covariance alignment,
following the implementation of the teacher codebase
\cite{zoumpourlis2024motor}. The Riemannian geometry of covariance
matrices is widely used in cross-subject motor imagery transfer to
cope with inter-subject variability \cite{xu2021selective}. For every subject, and separately within each of the training,
validation, and test partitions, a spatial covariance matrix is estimated
per trial with the OAS estimator. These matrices are averaged across the
trials of that subject using the Riemannian (affine-invariant) mean. The
mean defines a whitening transform ($\mathrm{cov}^{-1/2}$), which is
applied to every trial of that subject in that partition. This is the only step beyond unit
conversion that touches the amplitude/covariance structure of the
signal.

This alignment step has a direct consequence for how the reported
accuracy should be interpreted. The whitening transform for a given test subject is estimated from that
subject's own test-set trials, so the evaluation protocol is
\emph{offline and transductive at the subject level}. Aligning one test
trial uses second-order statistics pooled over all of that subject's test
trials. In an online single-command deployment, some of these trials would
not yet be available at the time of inference. This step neither mixes
information across subjects nor uses test \emph{labels}. It is therefore
not train/test leakage in the usual sense, in which preprocessing is
fitted jointly on training and test data \cite{kapoor2023leakage}. The
accuracy figures in this paper quantify decoding when a batch of unlabeled
data from the same session is available for alignment. They do not
quantify single-window decoding for a new user with no prior data.
Supporting an online or inductive deployment claim directly would require
estimating this transform only from data available before each inference.
Examples are a calibration block recorded at the start of a session or an
online running estimate. Such a protocol has not been run in this study. The
Pi 4 latency measurement in Section~\ref{sec:danhgiadk} times only
the network's forward pass and does not include the cost of
estimating or applying this alignment step.

The evaluation protocol is 5-fold cross-validation split by subject,
to ensure that no subject appears simultaneously in the training set
and the test set. This setup reflects real deployment conditions,
where the system must operate with users who never participated in
training.

Folds are split according to a deterministic rule. All 103 valid
subjects, keeping their code order, are divided into 5 consecutive,
nearly equal blocks. For each fold index, one block is chosen as the
test set, the next block in cyclic order as the validation set, and
the remaining three blocks as the training set. The corresponding split
sizes are approximately 62 subjects for training, 21 for
validation, and 20 for testing. The validation set is always completely
separated by subject from both other sets. This split does not
depend on the random seed. The same fold index always yields the
same set of subjects across all five seeds. The seed governs only the randomness of training (weight initialization,
batch order, and class-balancing undersampling), not how the data is
split. The complete list of 103 subject-to-fold
assignments is provided with the published source code but is not
printed in the paper because of its length.

The entire procedure is repeated with five independent random seeds.
The accuracy of each configuration is computed with the subject, not the
trial or the fold, as the statistical unit. This avoids the overly
optimistic evaluation caused by the high correlation among samples within
the same subject. Each subject enters the test
set exactly once across the 5 folds, so each configuration is
aggregated over $5 \times 103 = 515$ per-subject results.

These results are not independent of one another, because subjects
within the same fold of the same seed share a single student model.
The unit of inference for hypothesis tests is therefore the seed. Each
seed reduces the 515 results to a single mean over subjects. Every
paired $t$-test in the paper uses $n = 5$ independent pairs, with
$df = 4$ degrees of freedom. The ``mean $\pm$ standard deviation''
values in the tables are likewise computed over these five per-seed
values. Alongside $p$ values, the paper reports differences with 95\%
confidence intervals and Cohen's $d_z$ of the paired difference. This
choice of unit confirms whether a conclusion is robust to the
randomization of training. It does not confirm generalization to a
different subject population, because every seed is evaluated on the
same 103 subjects. The generalization question is examined separately
through an exploratory replication on Cho2017. The statistical plan
for the main kernel comparison, including the number of seeds, the
test, and the $\pm 0.5$ percentage-point equivalence margin, was
recorded in an internal analysis plan written before running. This plan
was not registered on a public registry and is released with the code.
The $\pm 0.5$-point margin was not derived from a power calculation.
Examined after running, it corresponds to about $0.2$ bit/min of ITR at
the observed accuracy level (from 5.97 to 6.40 bit/min around 6.18
bit/min, see Section~\ref{sec:rangbuoc}). It also exceeds the largest deviation between two reruns of the same configuration ($0.37$ points,
Section~\ref{sec:xephang} and Appendix~\ref{app:giaithua}).

\subsection{Control-oriented evaluation criteria}
\label{sec:rangbuoc}

Besides classification accuracy, the paper evaluates configurations
using quantities of the control loop. Information transfer rate
measures the bandwidth of the control channel \cite{wolpaw2002brain}.
For an $N$-class problem with accuracy $P$, the amount of information
obtained per command is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:itr-bits}
B = \log_2 N + P\log_2 P + (1-P)\log_2\!\left(\frac{1-P}{N-1}\right),
\end{equation}

and the information transfer rate in bits per minute is

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:itr-rate}
\mathrm{ITR} = B \times \frac{60}{T},
\end{equation}

where $T$ is the time required for one command, in seconds. For the
two-class case, expression \eqref{eq:itr-bits} reduces to
$B = 1 - H(P)$, where $H$ is the binary entropy function. In the
mid-accuracy range, a small improvement in accuracy produces a
bandwidth gain much larger than the corresponding ratio. ITR computed
via \eqref{eq:itr-rate} relies on several assumptions about operating
conditions and can give biased estimates if applied carelessly in
online systems \cite{yuan2013study}.

The time $T$ does not consist solely of the signal window length. The
time budget of the closed loop is decomposed as

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:latency}
T_{\mathrm{loop}} = T_{\mathrm{window}} + T_{\mathrm{infer}}
                  + T_{\mathrm{command}},
\end{equation}

where $T_{\mathrm{window}}$ is the length of the signal segment to be acquired and
$T_{\mathrm{infer}}$ is the model's inference time. The last term,
$T_{\mathrm{command}}$, is the time to transmit and execute the command on
the actuator. Model design directly affects only $T_{\mathrm{infer}}$,
which is also the term most often neglected in offline evaluation studies.

Cohen's kappa coefficient is often preferred in BCI evaluation
because it corrects for chance agreement. This advantage only takes
effect when the classes are imbalanced or number more than two. For a
two-class problem with exactly equal class counts, kappa is a
deterministic linear transformation of accuracy
($\kappa = 2\,\mathrm{acc} - 1$) and carries no additional
information. The two classes here are only approximately balanced in
the test set (Section~\ref{sec:dulieu}), so this identity holds
approximately. For this reason, kappa is not reported separately.

\subsection{Teacher and student network architectures}
\label{sec:kientruc}

The teacher--student pair was chosen to maximize the separation
between representational capacity and deployability. The teacher is
Ensemble-MI \cite{zoumpourlis2024motor}, a classification system
consisting of multiple convolutional branches operating in parallel.
It is trained using a curriculum and collaborative learning
mechanism. With about 15.1 thousand parameters, this model is about 6.3 times
larger than the student. Its inference is 7.1 times slower because
computational cost scales with the number of branches.
The teacher is trained separately for each fold, yielding 5
independent checkpoints corresponding exactly to the 5 data splits
in Section~\ref{sec:dulieu}. Each checkpoint is trained for 120
epochs with 7 collaborative-learning subsets, following the original
Ensemble-MI procedure exactly.

The student is EEGNet \cite{lawhern2018eegnet}, using depthwise and
separable convolutions to sharply reduce the number of parameters.
With about 2.4 thousand parameters (2,402, about 10.2 million MACs
per inference, see Section~\ref{sec:ketqua}), this architecture has
the potential to be deployed on a microcontroller. This parameter
scale is in the same class as a published EEGNet deployment on
embedded hardware \cite{wang2024mibminet}. It has not, however, been verified directly on
that class of ultra-low-power microcontroller. Section~\ref{sec:danhgiadk} reports a direct
inference-time measurement on real embedded hardware (Raspberry Pi
4). Both models produce intermediate feature vectors of the same
dimensionality $d = 144$, so the feature-level and relation-level
loss functions are applied directly without an intermediate
projection layer.

The teacher is pretrained, kept fixed, and has gradient updates
disabled throughout the entire distillation process. On this
dataset, the teacher achieves an accuracy of $84.98\% \pm 2.59\%$
(mean and standard deviation over 5 folds).

\subsection{Full training and evaluation pipeline}
\label{sec:pipeline}

Algorithm~2 gathers the steps described from Section~\ref{sec:dulieu}
to Section~\ref{sec:cauhinh} into a single procedure, corresponding to
Figure~\ref{fig:quytrinh}. With it, the reader can reproduce the order
of teacher training, fold construction, seed use, and validation-based
model selection without assembling information from several sections.

\begin{quote}
\ttfamily\small\raggedright
\noindent\textbf{Algorithm 2. Full training and evaluation pipeline.}\\[2pt]
\textbf{Input:} preprocessed data of the 103 subjects, configuration
set $\mathcal{C}$, seed set $\{1,\ldots,5\}$.\\
\textbf{Output:} per-seed values $\bar{a}_{c,s}$ and the test result
of each comparison.\\[4pt]
1: \quad Divide the 103 subjects, in code order, into 5 consecutive
blocks $B_0,\ldots,B_4$\\
2: \quad \textbf{for} $k = 0,\ldots,4$: test $\leftarrow B_k$,
validation $\leftarrow B_{(k+1) \bmod 5}$, training $\leftarrow$ the
remaining three blocks\\
3: \quad \textbf{for} $k = 0,\ldots,4$: Riemannian alignment per
subject within each partition of fold $k$\\
4: \quad \textbf{for} $k = 0,\ldots,4$: train teacher $T_k$ on the
training partition (120 epochs, fixed seed), keep the checkpoint with
the best validation accuracy, then freeze $T_k$\\[4pt]
5: \quad \textbf{for} each seed $s$, each fold $k$, each configuration
$c \in \mathcal{C}$:\\
6: \quad \quad set seed $s$ for weight initialization, batch order,
and sampling\\
7: \quad \quad class-balance the training trials of each subject by
undersampling\\
8: \quad \quad \textbf{for} epoch $= 1,\ldots,30$: update the student
via \eqref{eq:total} with the coefficients of $c$ (QRD branch as in
Section~\ref{sec:qrd}, V5 via Algorithm~1), $T_k$ runs forward passes
only\\
9: \quad \quad \quad measure accuracy on the validation and test
partitions\\
10: \quad \quad keep the per-subject test results at the epoch with
the best validation accuracy\\[4pt]
11: \quad \textbf{for} each $c$ and $s$: $\bar{a}_{c,s} \leftarrow$
mean over the 103 subjects (each subject is in the test set of exactly
one fold)\\
12: \quad \textbf{for} each pre-specified comparison $(c_1, c_2)$:
paired $t$-test on the 5 pairs $(\bar{a}_{c_1,s}, \bar{a}_{c_2,s})$,
95\% CI, and $d_z$
\end{quote}

Two properties of Algorithm~2 determine how the results should be
read. The fold split in lines 1--2 and the teacher in line 4 do not depend on
the seed. Every configuration and every seed thus share exactly the same
five splits and five teachers. The difference between
two configurations within the same seed therefore comes only from the
knowledge-transfer mechanism and from training noise. The test set
takes part in model selection at no step. Both the teacher and the
student are selected on the validation partition, which is fully
separated by subject from the test partition. The aggregation in line
11 reduces the 515 per-subject results to five values, matching the
unit of inference stated in Section~\ref{sec:dulieu}.

The independently trained student reaches 74.93\%, while every
distilled configuration reaches about 81\%. The improvement is 6.56
percentage points (95\% confidence interval from 4.08 to 9.04 points,
$p = 0.0018$, $d_z = 3.28$), far exceeding every other source of

choosing $\alpha = 1.0$ for the no-distillation configuration. Its

6.56 percentage points, whereas all five QRD variants together with
RelationalKD span only about 0.78 points around the KDBase mark.
Section~\ref{sec:chuyensau} analyzes each design axis behind this
variation.

\subsection{In-depth analysis of variant behavior}
\label{sec:chuyensau}

This part isolates each design axis of Table~\ref{tab:tongthe} and then
checks the sensitivity of the conclusion to the coefficient $\beta$ and
summarizes the other design controls.

\paragraph{Relation-formation strategy and kernel function type}

In the dimensionality-reduction block, V1 uses a deterministic,
parameter-free projection on both sides. V2 uses a frozen random $W_T$ on
the teacher side and a trained $W_S$ on the student side (580 parameters,
Table~\ref{tab:bienthe_cauhinh}). This comparison
is read as between two overall designs (Section~\ref{sec:bienthe}). V1
is $0.41$ percentage points above V2 in the direct comparison
($p = 0.085$), running counter to the design expectation but not
reaching significance. The learned projection shows no measurable
benefit over the deterministic design.

In the relation-strategy block, the three variants range from 81.12\%
to 81.42\%, and none separates from KDBase (smallest $p$ 0.231). The V5
strategy, expected to select the most informative relations, gives the
lowest result in the group. V5 differs from V2--V4 in both the
selection criterion and the candidate pool (64 versus 16 samples). The
control variant \emph{random\_pool64} keeps the 64-sample pool but
selects randomly, reaching $81.36\%$, not significantly different from
either V2 or V5. The available data therefore do not rule out either
factor.

In the kernel block, the V5 strategy is fixed to isolate the effect of
the kernel alone. The three kernels span 0.51 percentage points, with
cosine at 81.63\%, RBF at 81.61\%, and quantum at 81.12\%. The paired
$t$-test over five seeds, following the protocol fixed in advance, gives
$p = 0.096$ (cosine minus quantum) and $p = 0.060$ (RBF minus quantum).
Subjects within the same fold and seed share one model, and the five
seeds are evaluated on the same subjects. The comparison was therefore also
fitted with a mixed-effects regression with crossed random effects for
subject and seed, and a subject-clustered bootstrap (10,000 iterations)
gave the same range of results. Cosine minus quantum
is $0.50$ points (95\% confidence interval $[0.00, 1.01]$,
$p \approx 0.05$), and RBF minus quantum is $0.49$ points
($[-0.01, 0.99]$, $p \approx 0.05$--$0.06$). The result of the
pre-specified test is not superseded by these additional analyses. The
two comparisons belong to one family, so a minimal Bonferroni
correction brings the threshold to $0.025$, which no $p$ value reaches.
The observed effect size nearly coincides with the $\pm 0.5$-point
equivalence margin. The correct conclusion under the rules fixed in
advance is \emph{not enough data to conclude in either direction},
neither equivalence nor a significant difference. Cosine and RBF do not
differ ($p = 0.920$).

\paragraph{The coefficient $\beta$ and the effective supervisory magnitude}

The coefficient $\beta$ is checked in three steps. V5 Guided at
$\beta = 0.01$ and $\beta = 0.05$ reaches $81.47\%$ and $81.42\%$,
neither separating from KDBase ($p = 0.91$ and $p = 0.82$) nor differing
from the nominal level ($p = 0.15$ and $p = 0.40$). A gradient diagnostic on one fold and the first five epochs shows that
the same $\beta = 0.02$ produces very different student-gradient norms
(Appendix~\ref{app:kiemsoat}). The norm is about 20 times larger for RBF
than for cosine and about 200 times larger for quantum than for cosine. The three kernels therefore
do not receive equivalent supervision when sharing $\beta$. To test the effect of this disparity directly, an additional experiment
with a statistical plan fixed before running scaled $\beta$ to $4.0$ for
cosine and $0.2$ for RBF. These values bring their initial gradient
magnitude level with that of the quantum kernel at $\beta = 0.02$. The experiment also
swept $\beta \in \{0.1, 0.5\}$ for all three kernels. The pre-specified
main comparison is quantum ($\beta = 0.02$) versus cosine
($\beta = 4.0$). The difference is $-0.16$ percentage points (95\%
confidence interval $[-0.51, +0.20]$, $p = 0.279$, $d_z = -0.56$). The
lower bound exceeds the $-0.5$-point margin by about $0.01$ points, so
under the rules fixed in advance the conclusion remains not enough
data. Quantum versus RBF at $\beta = 0.2$ differs by $+0.13$ points
($p = 0.56$). All twelve configurations of this experiment lie between
81.32\% and 81.87\%, and no kernel shows a trend with $\beta$
(Appendix~\ref{app:kiemsoat}). Because the earlier numbers could not be
reproduced exactly (Section~\ref{sec:xephang}), every comparison in this
experiment uses only configurations rerun in the same batch.
Rebalancing the supervisory magnitude does not change the conclusion
about the kernel.

\paragraph{Interaction between kernel and relation strategy}

The comparisons above vary one axis at a time. To test whether the effect of the kernel depends on the relation
strategy, an additional experiment crossed the three kernels with
strategies V2--V5 (12 cells, $\beta = 0.02$, seeds 1--5). Its analysis
plan was written before running, and the full results are in
Appendix~\ref{app:giaithua}. The pre-specified test is the kernel
$\times$ strategy interaction in a two-way repeated-measures analysis of
variance over seeds. The interaction is not significant
($F(6, 24) = 0.87$, $p = 0.53$). This result means that no interaction
was detected at the power of a five-seed design. It does not show that
the two axes are independent. Descriptively, the quantum kernel is below
both classical kernels under all four strategies, by 0.06 to 0.60
points. However, none of these eight differences is significant
(smallest $p = 0.065$), and the main effect of the kernel does not reach
significance either ($F(2, 8) = 4.12$, $p = 0.059$).

\paragraph{Other design controls}

Three other design controls are reported in full in
Appendix~\ref{app:doichung}. When cosine and RBF are recomputed on the
projected space used by the quantum branch, the gap to the quantum kernel
narrows to 0.13--0.24 points instead of widening. Fixing or splitting the
shared circuit parameters $\theta$ does not change accuracy significantly
($p \geq 0.14$). Fixing $W_T$ across seeds lowers the seed standard
deviation of V2 from 0.78 to 0.51 points without significantly changing
the mean. The same appendix includes a t-SNE analysis of the student
features, whose result is in the same direction as the accuracy gap
between students with and without distillation.

\subsection{Seed noise and the stability of the method ranking}
\label{sec:xephang}

No relation variant or kernel function separated from KDBase in the
part above. Is this because the true difference among mechanisms is small,
or because seed noise exceeds the detection capability of the protocol?
The standard deviation of the mean values across the twelve
distillation mechanisms is 0.459 percentage points. The average standard
deviation from changing the seed within the same mechanism is 0.750
points. The ratio between the two is 0.61. FeatureKD alone, the only
mechanism with a significant effect, contributes 75\% of the total sum
of squared deviations among the twelve means. Excluding FeatureKD, the standard deviation across the remaining eleven
mechanisms is 0.205 points, against a seed standard deviation of 0.726
points for the same group. The ratio then drops to 0.28. The gap among
mechanisms is smaller
than the noise that the training procedure itself generates.

\subsection{Discussion}
\label{sec:thaoluan}

\textbf{(i) Main findings.} Under the 30-epoch training budget, knowledge
distillation provides a large and replicable benefit, 6.56 percentage points on PhysionetMI and 2.22 points
on Cho2017. Once distillation is present, the differences among the
twelve specific mechanisms lie within the noise of the training process
itself. The ranking from a single run is almost uncorrelated with the
ranking over five seeds ($\rho = 0.14$). Two batches of the same
configuration also deviate by up to 0.37 points. The quantum kernel, the strictest test case, does not separate from the
classical kernels, even after the supervisory magnitude is rebalanced.
Under the rules fixed in advance, this comparison remains inconclusive
rather than showing equivalence. The factorial experiment also detected no
kernel $\times$ strategy interaction, within the power of a five-seed
design.

\textbf{(ii) Comparison with other studies.} The observed seed noise is
consistent with warnings from computer vision and machine learning in
general \cite{picard2021torch, bouthillier2021accounting} and from deep
learning on neuroimaging data \cite{delpup2024toward}. This study extends
those warnings to the comparison of distillation mechanisms for motor
imagery EEG, with concrete measurements of ranking stability. A
real-world evaluation of ten deep motor imagery decoders under a soft
real-time protocol likewise found that rankings shifted between offline and online settings
\cite{sedinzakuna2025realworld}. In that study, inter-subject variability
also increased markedly in the online setting. Together with the seed-induced ranking
instability measured here, this suggests that offline single-run rankings
should be treated as provisional. Earlier
relational distillation work for EEG proposed new mechanisms within its
own setting \cite{huang2024enhancing, wu2026multilevel}. The results
here do not directly contradict that work, because the datasets,
teachers, and protocols differ. Even so, differences of a few tenths of
a percentage point among mechanisms lie within the noise floor measured
here. They are hard to detect reliably from a single run. For the
quantum kernel, the absence of an advantage is consistent with the
observation that classical models learning from data can match quantum
models \cite{huang2021power}. Quantum relational distillation was
proposed and evaluated on vision and language \cite{liu2025quantum}. On
EEG with the protocol used here, it does not separate from the classical
kernels. Embedded deployment studies compare CPU, GPU, and FPGA platforms
by inference time, power, and memory \cite{pacini2024design} or
demonstrate real-time EEG decoding on edge devices
\cite{sen2025lowlatency}. The Pi 4 measurement here is narrower, covering
only the forward pass of the student. The absolute accuracies in this paper are measured under a
subject-level transductive protocol (Section~\ref{sec:dulieu}), so they
are not directly comparable with figures obtained under online or
inductive protocols.

The directly comparable numerical reference is the teacher study
itself \cite{zoumpourlis2024motor}. That study uses the same 103
PhysionetMI subjects, the same two-class task, the same preprocessing
code base, and the same subject-wise 5-fold split into training,
validation, and test blocks. It reports 86.36\% for the ensemble with
seven first-stage models and 82.09\% for a single EEGNet trained for
120 epochs with a learning-rate schedule. The teacher reproduced in this
paper reaches 84.98\%, 1.38 percentage points lower. The distilled
students in Table~\ref{tab:tongthe} (81.12\% to 81.90\%) remain below
that single EEGNet. The no-distillation student (74.93\%) is about 7
points below it. The training budget explains at least part of this
gap. The students here are trained for 30 epochs at a fixed learning
rate of 0.005 on 3-s windows. The reference recipe uses 120 epochs, a
decaying learning rate, and 4-s windows. The teacher here is also
trained on 3-s windows. The 6.56-point benefit of distillation is
therefore measured relative to a student trained under a short budget
and should not be read as the gain over a fully tuned EEGNet. This does
not affect the comparisons among mechanisms, because every mechanism
shares the same budget. It does, however, limit the claims about
absolute accuracy.

\textbf{(iii) Interpretation and implications.} Once logit distillation
is present, additional information from relations among samples appears
to contribute little to final accuracy, whether relations are measured in
Euclidean, cosine, RBF, or Hilbert space. That this pattern also holds for the quantum kernel suggests that the
limited role of relation structure is not specific to one representation
space, at least among the four spaces examined. This does not mean the representation space plays
no role at all, a stronger claim than the data allow. For system design,
the choice of specific distillation mechanism changes control-channel
bandwidth by only about 0.33 bit/min when FeatureKD is excluded, which is unlikely to be operationally
meaningful. Training cost and implementation complexity are therefore more
useful selection criteria. With
the current implementation, the roughly 13-fold cost of the quantum
branch is not accompanied by a measurable benefit. Methodologically,
comparisons of distillation mechanisms for EEG need multiple seeds,
confidence intervals, and checks of the deviation between reruns, rather
than a single score.

\textbf{(iv) Strengths and limitations.} The strengths of the study include the subject as the statistical unit,
a statistical plan fixed in advance for the kernel comparisons, and design
controls. They also include an exploratory replication on a second dataset
and a measurement on real embedded hardware. The first limitation is that per-subject covariance alignment
makes the protocol offline and transductive at the subject level. The
accuracy, ITR, and latency figures should be read under this protocol.
Supporting an online deployment claim directly requires a protocol that
uses only data available before each inference. With five seeds, the minimum detectable effect at 80\% power among the
distilled mechanisms lies between about 0.65 and 1.35 percentage points
depending on the comparison (Appendix~\ref{app:mde}). This is larger than most
differences among mechanisms and larger than the $\pm 0.5$-point
equivalence margin. The student training budget (30 epochs, a fixed
learning rate, 3-s windows) is shorter than the reference recipe, so the
absolute accuracies are below the published single-EEGNet figure. The
main comparison is carried out
only on PhysionetMI with a two-class problem, and the survey on Cho2017
is exploratory. The quantum component takes only one circuit form with
four qubits run on a simulator, whereas the performance of quantum kernels
depends on the feature map \cite{jha2026comparative}. The conclusion
therefore does not extend to quantum machine learning in general. Non-deterministic training means
that the numbers are not reproducible to the last digit. The $\beta$
rebalancing ratio was measured early in training and may drift later.
The controls on $\theta$, $W_T$, and V5's candidate pool do not entirely
rule out the corresponding theoretical risks. The study has also not
been validated in a closed control loop with real users.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:ketluan}

This paper presents a controlled evaluation protocol for knowledge
distillation mechanisms on motor imagery EEG, using the QRD framework as
the test bed. The QRD framework separates the relation-formation
strategy from the representation space and keeps every relation kernel,
classical or quantum, outside the deployed model. Five strategies and
three kernel function types were compared under uniform conditions over
five seeds, with the subject as the statistical unit.

The study supports three conclusions. Under the 30-epoch training budget,
knowledge distillation provides a large benefit, which replicates in
direction on the exploratory Cho2017 dataset. The differences among
specific distillation mechanisms are smaller than the noise from seeds
and from reruns, large enough to reverse the method ranking between runs.
No quantum variant provides a statistically significant advantage over
the classical kernels, even after rebalancing the supervisory magnitude. The factorial
experiment detected no interaction between kernel and relation strategy
either. The paper does not establish equivalence between quantum and
classical kernels. The new contribution to the field is quantitative
evidence that, in this controlled setting, rankings of distillation
mechanisms for EEG drawn from a single run are unreliable. The study also
contributes a control-oriented evaluation procedure and a latency
measurement on embedded hardware.
This result does not refute published single-run results. It only
shows that differences of a few tenths of a percentage point need
confirmation over multiple seeds. The main limitations, including the
transductive protocol, five seeds, and a single main dataset, are stated
in Section~\ref{sec:thaoluan}.

Future work should evaluate an online protocol that uses only data
available before each inference. It should also validate the decoder in a
closed control loop with real users, measuring end-to-end latency, memory,
and energy. The main comparison should be repeated on a second dataset
with a publicly registered statistical plan. Extending the study to the
multi-class problem and to larger quantum circuits, when the
infrastructure allows, is a further direction. On the methodological front, a protocol that
determines the minimum number of repetitions from the measured noise
floor, together with deterministic training, would make method
comparisons in this field more reliable.

\textbf{Reproducibility.} The training, preprocessing, and statistical
analysis code, the list of 103 subject-to-fold assignments, the seeds,
the five teacher checkpoints, and the per-seed results are publicly available at
\url{https://github.com/nguyenphuonghuy/qrd_eeg} as of the submission of
this revision. The software environment is version-pinned (PyTorch
2.7.1, PennyLane, braindecode). Because GPU training is
non-deterministic, reruns deviate within the range shown in
Table~\ref{tab:taplap} and Appendix~\ref{app:giaithua}.

\section{Implementation details}
\label{app:caidat}

\textbf{Initialization of the projections.} Both $W_T$ and $W_S$ are PyTorch \texttt{nn.Linear} layers initialized
with the library's default distribution, without any further normalization
step. The weights follow a Kaiming-uniform distribution and the bias a
uniform distribution, both scaled by the input dimensionality. This initialization step occurs after several
other calls that consume random numbers (data loading, teacher and
student initialization) within the same global random number stream
seeded per run. As a result, $W_T$ is likely to differ across the
experiment's five seeds, an unintended consequence of the global
seeding scheme.

\textbf{Theoretical limits of the projection and angle encoding.} The
random linear projection at a $144 \to 4$ compression ratio has no
Johnson--Lindenstrauss-type theoretical guarantee of preserving
relation structure. For a circuit with multiple qubits and multiple
entangling layers, the injectivity of the overall mapping is also not
rigorously proven merely from bounding the angle domain in
\eqref{eq:angles}.

\textbf{Circuit degeneracy diagnostic.} The difference of $0.474$
between the quantum kernel and the corresponding cosine product is
compared with a threshold of $0.05$. This threshold is a small
conventional marker, used to detect complete degeneration into a
classical kernel (a difference near 0), and is not based on a formal
statistical test.

\textbf{Diagonal pairs in V1 and V2.} The index set
$\mathcal{I}_{\mathrm{V1}}$ includes the diagonal pairs $(i,i)$. These
elements always have $R_T^{(Q)}(i,i) = R_S^{(Q)}(i,i) = 1$ by the
definition of fidelity, so they contribute exactly 0 to
$\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{QRD}}$, carry no information, and generate no
gradient. They only slightly dilute the average in \eqref{eq:qrd} by
increasing $|\mathcal{I}|$.

\textbf{Tied distances in V4.} When \texttt{torch.topk} encounters
tied distances among neighbors, the order returned depends on
PyTorch's implementation (stable but with no statistically meaningful
priority). With continuous features as used here, the probability of
an exact distance tie is almost negligible on real data.

\section{Additional design controls}
\label{app:doichung}

This appendix gives the full results of the three design controls
summarized in Section~\ref{sec:chuyensau} and the qualitative analysis of
the feature space.

\paragraph{Input space of the classical kernels}

The gap between the quantum kernel and the two classical kernels in
Section~\ref{sec:chuyensau} might stem from a non-equivalent input space. Cosine and
RBF use the raw 144-dimensional features, whereas the quantum kernel
uses the space projected through $W_S$. Cosine and RBF were recomputed on features projected through the learned
$W_S$ and the fixed $W_T$, identical to the quantum branch. The results are
projected cosine $81.25\pm0.70\%$ and projected RBF $81.36\pm0.91\%$. Neither
differs significantly from the original version (cosine
$\Delta=-0.38$, $p=0.168$; RBF $\Delta=-0.25$, $p=0.293$) or from
quantum V5 (projected cosine $+0.13$, $p=0.647$; projected RBF $+0.24$,
$p=0.312$). The gap between quantum and the classical kernels narrows
to $0.13$--$0.24$ points rather than widening.

\paragraph{Co-adaptation of the shared circuit parameters ($\theta$)}

Sharing $\theta$ could let training flatten both kernels instead of
forcing the student to reproduce the teacher's relation structure
(Section~\ref{sec:qrd}). V2 Dense was trained further with $\theta$
entirely fixed (\emph{shared\_fixed}, $81.73\pm0.49\%$) and with a fixed
$\theta_T$ separated from a trainable $\theta_S$ (\emph{split},
$81.35\pm0.87\%$), each with 5 seeds $\times$ 5 folds. Compared with the
original shared $\theta$ (\emph{shared}, $81.49\pm0.78\%$), no pair
differs significantly ($p=0.143$, $0.615$, and $0.215$). This result
does not prove that the degeneracy risk never occurs. It only shows no
measurable effect on accuracy at the scale examined.

\paragraph{Initialization of $W_T$}

V2 Dense was retrained with $W_T$ fixed at a single value while still
sweeping all five seeds. The standard deviation across seeds drops from
$0.78$ to $0.51$ percentage points, while the mean accuracy does not
differ significantly ($81.20\%$ versus $81.49\%$, $p = 0.22$). A
substantial but not complete part of the seed noise in the quantum
branch comes from $W_T$ changing with the global seed. This control does
not yet measure the variation across multiple initializations of $W_T$
at the same training seed.

\paragraph{Qualitative analysis of the feature space (t-SNE)}

Figure~\ref{fig:tsne} visualizes the student's intermediate features

\section{Sensitivity of the no-distillation configuration to $\alpha$}
\label{app:alpha}

The no-distillation configuration was trained further at four other
values of $\alpha$. Table~\ref{tab:alphasweep} shows that accuracy rises
monotonically with $\alpha$, from near-chance level at $\alpha=0$ (no
supervisory signal from the true labels) to $74.93\%$ at $\alpha=1.0$. If
the distillation improvement were computed at $\alpha = 0.5$, the
independent student would reach only $63.06\%$ and the improvement would
rise to $18.43$ percentage points. This difference does not reflect two
comparable estimates of the same effect. It reflects that $\alpha = 0.5$
does not give the no-distillation configuration enough weight on the one
loss term it has. $\alpha = 1.0$ is consistent with the full-weight
principle applied to every other configuration. The standard deviation
across seeds also rises sharply as $\alpha$ decreases, reaching 5.03
points at $\alpha = 0.5$.

\section{Training cost as a function of the number of qubits}
\label{app:qubit}

A preliminary survey measures training time as a function of the number
of qubits ($n_{\text{qubits}} \in \{4, 8, 12, 16\}$, 1 fold, 3 epochs,
every other hyperparameter fixed) using V2 Dense. V2 and V5 have the same
measured cost because they select the same number of sample pairs for
the kernel, differing only in the selection criterion. The number of
qubits affects only the cost of each kernel evaluation, so the trend
measured on V2 can be extrapolated to the other variants for cost rather
than for accuracy. Table~\ref{tab:qubitcost} shows that cost increases
superlinearly. The multiple between consecutive levels (about
$2.8\times$, $1.8\times$, and $4.5\times$ for each 4-qubit step) is much
lower than the $16\times$ that statevector simulation predicts for the circuit part alone. This suggests that fixed costs (data loading, the
classical backbone, PennyLane overhead) still account for a substantial
share at this scale. At $n_{\text{qubits}}=16$, the measurement did not
complete 3 epochs within 20 minutes. A full run at this level is
estimated at about 65 hours for one configuration, beyond the capacity
of the current infrastructure. At 8 and 12 qubits, full accuracy was
measured for V2 Dense. No pair among the three qubit levels differs
significantly (including 8 versus 12, $p=0.491$). With $n=5$, this result
only shows that no trend with the number of qubits has been detected.

\section{Latency measurement details}
\label{app:dotre}

\textbf{HPC server CPU.} The measurement runs on a shared compute node of the HPC cluster with an
AMD EPYC 9224 CPU (24 cores per socket, 2 sockets, max clock
$\approx 3.7$ GHz). It uses 8 threads, the default number of CPUs allocated
to the process, with PyTorch 2.7.1, the default Intel oneAPI MKL BLAS
backend, float32 precision, and Linux kernel 5.14 (Enterprise Linux 9). The configuration was confirmed on the same node,
although a slight change in OS patches between measurements cannot be
ruled out. The student's 3.17 ms is on the same order of magnitude as the
2.95 ms reported by another EEGNet deployment on an ultra-low-power
microcontroller \cite{wang2024mibminet}. These are two different
architectures, datasets, and hardware types, so this similarity does not
prove equivalent deployment conditions. Inference time on a
microcontroller also depends on the number of MACs, intermediate RAM,
precision, the kernel library, and the specific microcontroller. The MACs
and peak activation in Table~\ref{tab:tre} are computed analytically from
the EEGNet architecture, not measured on hardware.

\textbf{Raspberry Pi 4.} The measurement uses the same protocol as on the server CPU (200
inferences after 20 warm-up runs, batch size one, no special thread-count
configuration). It was repeated over 5 independent runs and re-verified
with a sixth run, which gave a consistent result. The
student's latency ranges from $11.7$ to $15.0$ ms across all
measurements, at $42.3^\circ$C, without thermal throttling, with the CPU
frequency held at the maximum 1800 MHz. The measurement uses randomly
initialized weights, because inference time and memory depend only on
the architecture and tensor sizes. The teacher's peak RAM
($251.63 \pm 0.11$ MiB) is only about $4.1\%$ higher than the student's
even though the teacher has $6.3$ times as many parameters. Most of the
measured RAM comes from the fixed overhead of the Python interpreter and
the PyTorch runtime. This is the RAM of the entire process, which differs fundamentally from
the estimated $\approx 600$ KB of pure student activation. The two figures
should not be compared directly.

\section{Minimum detectable effects}
\label{app:mde}

Table~\ref{tab:mde} reports the minimum detectable effect (MDE) at 80\%
power for each comparison on PhysionetMI, with a two-sided paired $t$-test,
$\alpha = 0.05$, and $n = 5$ seeds, computed with the noncentral $t$
distribution. The analysis uses the observed standard deviation of the paired
difference, so it is a post hoc analysis rather than a sample-size calculation
made before running. Among the distilled mechanisms, the MDE lies between
0.65 and 1.35 percentage points depending on the standard deviation of
each pair. For the three kernel
comparisons and V1 versus V2, the MDE ranges from 0.68 to 0.87 points, all
larger than the $\pm 0.5$-point equivalence margin. The five-seed design
therefore lacks the power to detect reliably a difference of the size of the
equivalence margin, consistent with the not-enough-data conclusion in
Section~\ref{sec:chuyensau}.
